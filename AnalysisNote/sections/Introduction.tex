\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Machine learning has been central to high-energy physics for decades, enabling
reconstruction algorithms, event classification, and detector optimization across
collider experiments. However, a new frontier has emerged with the recent capability
of large language models (LLMs) developed by major AI labs---including
Anthropic~\cite{Anthropic:2025accelerating}, OpenAI~\cite{OpenAI:2025gpt5science},
Google DeepMind~\cite{GoogleDeepMind:2025geminiscience}, and others---to assist directly
with scientific reasoning, mathematical proof, and code generation. In this emerging
domain, there exists a striking asymmetry in fundamental physics: nearly all current
applications of LLMs to accelerate scientific progress operate on the theoretical or
computational side. Recent examples include LLM-assisted derivations of new results in
theoretical particle physics~\cite{OpenAI:2025newresult}, design of novel protein
structures for biology and health research~\cite{GoogleDeepMind:2025alphaproteo}, and
assistance with complex mathematical simplifications in high-energy physics calculations.
The experimental side of the AI-assisted scientific loop remains largely absent. This
asymmetry constrains discovery. To realize a future where AI discovers new physics,
LLM-assisted scientific workflows must be developed and validated across \textit{both}
precision theory \textit{and} precision experiment. Such frameworks, operating across
the theory--experiment interface, would have the capacity not only to propose new
theoretical models or observables, but to design experimental tests of those predictions
and hold them accountable to measured data.

This vision parallels the evolution of AI in neighboring scientific domains. In materials
science and structural biology, AI work has progressed from computational prediction with
post-hoc human validation in the lab, toward AI-designed experiments executed by autonomous
robotic systems. This creates closed feedback loops where AI proposes, executes, measures,
and learns. The key enabler was developing AI systems that understood both the computational
theory space and the experimental measurement space. High-energy physics faces a parallel
opportunity. While we cannot deploy robotic particle detectors, the wealth of open, curated
data from past experiments---particularly the precision LEP dataset---can serve as a
``pseudo-laboratory'' for developing and validating LLM-assisted workflows that operate
across the precision theory--experiment interface. This requires experimental infrastructure
on both sides: precision theoretical predictions and precision experimental measurements
with rigorous covariance quantification, where LLM-assisted reasoning is tested and held
accountable to measurable constraints.

Achieving Standard Model predictions at the precision required for future collider programs,
including the High-Luminosity LHC, demands QCD calculations with uncertainty control
matched to percent-level experimental precision. In this regime, precision experimental
measurement becomes validation infrastructure for AI models. When percent-level uncertainties
are coupled to rigorous covariance quantification and end-to-end reproducibility, high-precision
measurements define the testing ground where AI predictions are made quantitative, falsifiable,
and subject to rigorous scientific accountability~\cite{Wang:2023aai,Messeri:2024aiu,Woodruff:2026practicalsi}.

Precision QCD can be pursued at hadron colliders, but tests are most cleanly controlled in
lepton-collider environments with well-defined initial states. Although there is currently
no active high-energy lepton collider, archived LEP data now provide an unprecedented
open-data opportunity for precision event-shape studies with explicit uncertainty control
and covariance quantification~\cite{EEAlliance:website,Badea:2019vey,Chen:2021uws}. Relative
to hadron- and nuclear-collision open-data settings, the LEP $e^{+}e^{-}$ environment provides
substantially tighter control of soft-event complexity and initial-state structure, making it
a uniquely clean benchmark for both precision QCD and precision AI model development.

Event-shape observables measured at LEP continue to anchor comparisons between perturbative calculations, generator modeling, and non-perturbative hadronization constraints. Among these observables, thrust is a primary precision test because it probes both perturbative radiation and non-perturbative dynamics in a single distribution~\cite{PhysRevLett.39.1587,Abbate:2010xh,Heister:2003aj}. Recent thrust-based extractions report $\alpha_s(m_Z)\simeq 0.1136\pm 0.0012$, below the 2023 world-average value $\alpha_s(m_Z)=0.1180\pm 0.0009$~\cite{Benitez:2024nav,ParticleDataGroup:2022pth}. This persistent tension, often referred to as the $e^{+}e^{-}$ $\alpha_s$ puzzle, sharpens the need for renewed measurements with explicit uncertainty control and publishable covariance information. The thrust peak and logarithmic moments are also increasingly important for constraining non-perturbative shape-function effects~\cite{Abbate:2010xh,Assi:2025ibi}.

This analysis is part of a broader AI-for-science program where precision is non-negotiable. AI systems are increasingly used to assist theoretical physics calculations and model construction~\cite{Carleo:2019mlphys,Karagiorgi:2022mlnewphysics,Cranmer:2023lqcdml,Gukov:2024rigorml}, while agent-oriented scientific workflows are developing rapidly in both autonomous laboratory settings and LLM-based discovery frameworks~\cite{Abolhasani:2023sdl,MacLeod:2020sdl,Chen:2024scienceagentbench,Lu:2024aiscientist}. Recent AI+scientist studies emphasize recurring workflow patterns, including problem decomposition, iterative refinement with tool feedback, and adversarial-style verification loops, while also showing that domain-expert validation remains the limiting requirement for scientific reliability~\cite{Woodruff:2026practicalsi,Bubeck:2025coscientist}. In high-energy theory, early case studies now include AI-assisted amplitude and event-shape calculations~\cite{OpenAITheory:2026singleminus,Schwartz:2026cparameterllm}. Physics utility, however, requires direct confrontation with experimental data. The curated LEP open-data environment enables a concrete theory--experiment loop in which AI-assisted calculations, observables, or model variants can be validated against high-precision measurements under domain-expert control.

Within that program, the immediate objective of this note is to establish a robust detector-corrected thrust baseline using Iterative Bayesian Unfolding (IBU) implemented in RooUnfold, with complete validation and systematic propagation in a workflow that can be iterated rapidly. In this stage, thrust is unfolded and validated end-to-end, including covariance and correlation products intended for downstream phenomenology use. In the next stage, the same framework will be extended to additional thrust-family observables (C-parameter and heavy-jet mass), and multidimensional unfolding requirements will be assessed.

A central part of this note is the reconstruction-level validation of the particle inputs that enter thrust. Since thrust is computed from selected charged and neutral particles, the modeling of particle-category kinematics directly impacts migration, regularization behavior, and final unfolded uncertainties. For this reason, the note includes detailed \texttt{pwflag}-resolved kinematic comparisons between data and simulation before the unfolding stage.
